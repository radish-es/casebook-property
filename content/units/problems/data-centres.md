### Real & Hypothetical Facts

Not since the forges and factories of the industrial revolution have large development projects provoked the kind of backlash we are starting to see in communities where gigantic data centres are being planned and built. Legal claims to slow or prevent these developments are on the rise, as local residents argue that property and people are increasingly under threat.

Review the facts reported in this article about the operation of a data centre called DataOne in Vineland, New Jersey, published by *[The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/28/datacenters-sound-pollution-lawsuits)* newspaper. In addition to those reported facts, suppose that the residents complaining of noise and vibration from DataOne moved nearby only recently, attracted by the earlier development of the land as a golf course--though some of those residents were apparently aware at the time when they moved that plans for construction of a new data centre in the area were in the works. The DataOne development is located on the outskirts of town in an area of mostly cornfields and cowsheds, but the Vineland town council has recently amended the zoning regulations for the area, designating it as "mixed use agricultural and industrial". The town has been actively pursuing other data centre developers to build in the area.

As noted in the *Guardian* article, in addition to noise, DataOne appears to be emitting serious vibrations. Some of those vibrations have been regularly shaking the nearby homes and in one instance, a resident claims that they started to find new cracks in the foundation of their garden shed only after the data centre began operations. 

Suppose also that the advocacy group Quiet Communities Inc. has started looking into the land title records for the area on which DataOne built its Vineland centre. They've discovered what appears to be a covenant attached to DataOne's title stipulating that the land "be used for organic food production". A small area on the land is apparently where DataOne plans to produce the "weekly baskets of organic, non-GMO fruits and vegetables" mentioned in the *Guardian* article. The covenant existed on title at the time when DataOne purchased the land from the farmer who previously owned it and had briefly operated the new golf course there, which was quickly abandoned for reasons unknown. The farmer mentioned in passing to DataOne's lawyers when they were negotiating the purchase that there were some "sustainability constraints" around using the land to ensure supply of organic vegetables to a small farm market on a nearby plot owned by a neighbour--and since sold to the developer of a new supermarket--but did not elaborate further.

### The Problem

**What legal claims, if any, might parties in this scenario bring against DataOne to address the problems described in the *Guardian* article, what are the chances that those claims will succeed, and what would be the likely result?**

*For the purposes of answering this problem, assume that all relevant facts take place in Canada.*

### Guide to Reading and Analysis

This week we return to practicing the skills of issue spotting and legal analysis of a hypothetical fact pattern. There are essentially two issue areas to cover: a claim in nuisance and a claim related to the enforcement of a restrictive covenant (two different "private" common law land-use tools). It's possible to start your analysis with either issue area--but for simplicity, we'll address the nuisance claim first and then move on to the question of restrictive covenants.
