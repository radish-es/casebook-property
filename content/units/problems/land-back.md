### Hypothetical Facts

Suppose that a testator (the term for a person making a will, sometimes called a "testamentary bequest") owns a tract of farmland in Nova Scotia that has used by their their family as a commercial apple orchard for several generations. The land is valuable, likely worth somewhere close to $2 million on the open market. The testator has a daughter who has always wanted to farm and the testator has told others that they are keen to support this dream. But having recently attended a public lecture about the *Land Back* movement---an Indigenous-led movement advocating for the return of Indigenous lands to Indigenous peoples---and having followed the progress of claims by the [Wolastoqiyik](https://wnnb.wolastoqey.ca/the-fight-for-our-homeland-will-continue-wolastoqey-chief/) and the [Mi'kmaq](https://www.aptnnews.ca/featured/migmaq-communities-file-notice-of-action-against-new-brunswick-to-assert-their-land-rights/) for recognition and affirmation of their title to lands in New Brunswick in the news, the testator has developed an avid interest---if somewhat limited understanding---around land justice and repairing land relations. The testator instructs their lawyer to include the following provision in their Last Will & Testament:

> When I die, the farm should go to my daughter. When she can no longer make use of the land, it should go to Mi’gmawe’l Tplu’taqnn Inc. (MTI), which Mi'kmaq-led non-profit organization can use the land for producing food but is prohibited from transferring the land except to another Mi'kmaq-led entity for no more than an amount of $500,000. 

Other than the daughter named in the will, the testator has no other lawful heirs. Soon after the testator dies, the Government of Nova Scotia passes new legislation rendering void and of no effect any testamentary bequest that "restrains alienation of land". Where the testator of such a bequest has no lawful heirs, the land subject to the bequest escheats to the provincial Crown.  

### The Problem

**After the testator passes away but before the new legislation is passed, what interests in the farmland would likely be created through this provision in the will? Be sure to identify and address any problems with interpreting the will. Do you think a bequest of this nature addresses the harms of settler colonialism advanced by the *St. Catherine's Milling* decision and its aftermath? Is the bequest consistent with the aims of the Land Back movement as described by the Yellowhead Institute? Would the Province be liable to pay compensation to MTI for changes made via the new legislation?**

{{% step "Understanding Feudal Land Tenure and Contemporary Estates" %}}

We will begin this unit with some basic elements of Anglo-Canadian land law, in particular the system of tenure and estates in land that reaches all the way back to the Norman invasion of Anlgo-Saxon England in the eleventh century and evolved into the system of feudal relations that continues to provide the "superstructure" for the law today. Start by reading the overview materials on [feudal tenure]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/tenure" >}}) and [estates in land]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/basic-estates" >}}), and the *[Escheats Act]({{< ref "/casebook/tenure-estates/escheats-act" >}})*, followed by the cases and legislation that will help you to identify and characterize contemporary estates in land: *[Re Walker]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/walker" >}})*, *[Stuartburn v Kiansky]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/stuartburn" >}})*, *[Blackburn v McCallum]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/blackburn" >}})* and excerpts from the *[Conveyancing Act]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/conveyancing-act" >}})* and *[Wills Act]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/wills-act" >}})*.

{{% step "Eminent Domain and Compensation for State Expropriation" %}}

A second aspect the the lines of property and state power that were drawn during the late nineteenth century was the uncertain legal principles and constraints around the state's power to "take" private property, with or without paying fair compensation. We will look at a widely publicized dispute between two lumber businesses that tested the very foundations of early federalism in Canada: *[McLaren v Caldwell]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/mclaren" >}})* and the *[House of Commons Debates]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/house-debates" >}})* concerning the case, along with contemporary expropriation legislation in Nova Scotia, the *[Expropriation Act]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/expropriation-act" >}})*.
