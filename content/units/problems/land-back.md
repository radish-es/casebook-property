### Hypothetical Facts

Suppose that a "testator" (the term for a person making a will or "testamentary bequest") owns a tract of farmland in Nova Scotia that has been used by their their family as a commercial apple orchard for generations. The land is valuable, likely worth somewhere close to $2 million on the open market. The testator has no heirs but has a friend who has always wanted to farm and the testator has told this friend that they are keen to support their dream. But having recently attended a public lecture about the *Land Back* movement---an Indigenous-led movement advocating for the return of Indigenous lands to Indigenous peoples---and having followed the progress of claims by the [Wolastoqiyik](https://wnnb.wolastoqey.ca/the-fight-for-our-homeland-will-continue-wolastoqey-chief/) and the [Mi'kmaq](https://www.aptnnews.ca/featured/migmaq-communities-file-notice-of-action-against-new-brunswick-to-assert-their-land-rights/) for recognition and affirmation of their title to lands in New Brunswick, the testator has also developed an avid interest in land justice and repairing land relations. The testator instructs their lawyer to include the following provision in their Last Will & Testament:

> When I die, the farm should go to my friend. When she can no longer make use of the land, it should go to Mi’gmawe’l Tplu’taqnn Inc. (MTI), which Mi'kmaq-led non-profit organization is prohibited from transferring the land except to another Mi'kmaq-led entity and for no more than an amount of $500,000. 

Soon after the testator dies, the Government of Nova Scotia passes new farmland legislation that aims to address the declining availability and affordability of agricultural land in the province. The legislation declares that any testamentary bequest made within the last five years "which restrains, or attempts to restrain, alienation of land is unlawful and of no effect". The new legislation then goes on to prohibit any farmland inherited or otherwise transferred in the last five years from being converted to non-farm land uses. 

### The Problem

**After the testator dies but before the new legislation is passed, what interests in the farmland would likely be created through the above provision in the will? Be sure to identify and address any problems with interpreting the will. Do you think a bequest of this nature addresses the harms of settler colonialism advanced by the *St. Catherine's Milling* decision and its aftermath? Would the Province be liable to pay compensation to anyone for the consequences brought about by the new legislation?**

{{% step "Understanding Feudal Land Tenure and Contemporary Estates" %}}

We will begin this unit with some basic elements of Anglo-Canadian land law, in particular the system of tenure and estates in land that reaches all the way back to the Norman invasion of Anlgo-Saxon England in the eleventh century and evolved into the system of feudal relations that continues to provide the "superstructure" for the law today. Start by reading the overview materials on [feudal tenure]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/tenure" >}}) and [estates in land]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/basic-estates" >}}), and the *[Escheats Act]({{< ref "/casebook/tenure-estates/escheats-act" >}})*, followed by the cases and legislation that will help you to identify and characterize contemporary estates in land: *[Re Walker]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/walker" >}})*, *[Stuartburn v Kiansky]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/stuartburn" >}})*, *[Blackburn v McCallum]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/blackburn" >}})* and excerpts from the *[Conveyancing Act]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/conveyancing-act" >}})* and *[Wills Act]({{< ref "casebook/tenure-estates/wills-act" >}})*.

{{% step "Indigenous Land Rights, Title and Governance During the Rise of Settler Colonialism" %}}

Based on the understanding you gain from the readings above, we will explore how these structures have been used in the dispossession and denial of Indigenous land rights. While those dynamics did not start in the nineteenth century, we will look closely at a key legal developments during this period and into the early twentieth century. Start by reviewing the *[Royal Proclamation]({{< ref "casebook/indigenous/royal-proclamation" >}})* you read last unit, followed by *[St. Catherine's Milling]({{< ref "casebook/indigenous/stcatherines" >}})*, *[R v Syliboy]({{< ref "casebook/indigenous/syliboy" >}})*, the note on *[terra nullius]({{< ref "casebook/indigenous/terra-nullius" >}})* and the policy paper on [Land Back from the Yellowhead Institute]({{< ref "casebook/indigenous/yellowhead-land" >}}).

At this point, please also read by [Beals v Nova Scotia]({{< ref "casebook/registration/beals" >}}), focusing on the historical background around the settlement of African Nova Scotians. While the issues and historical context regarding the land relations of African Nova Scotians are distinct from those of Indigenous peoples, there is nevertheless an important connection between them that runs through the colonial logics of possession and dispossession prevalent during this period.

{{% step "Eminent Domain and Compensation for State Expropriation" %}}

A second aspect of property and state power that were drawn during the late nineteenth century were the uncertain legal principles and constraints around the state's ability to "take" private property, with or without paying fair compensation. We will look at a widely publicized dispute between two lumber businesses that tested the very foundations of early federalism in Canada: *[McLaren v Caldwell]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/mclaren" >}})* and the *[House of Commons Debates]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/house-debates" >}})* concerning the case. Then read *[Perry v Clissold]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/perry" >}})* on the issue of expropriation of a possessory interest and *[Pennsylvania Coal v Mahon]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/pennsylvania-coal" >}})* along with contemporary expropriation legislation in Nova Scotia, the *[Expropriation Act]({{< ref "casebook/expropriation/expropriation-act" >}})*.
